## Genesis Vault: The Artefact Beyond the Market

{{< square-image
     src="/images/verdictum/0026.webp"
     alt="VR-0020 — Appendix A. Visual standards"
     class="fade-in-up" >}}
  
#### An interview with Alexander Purpurov, founder of IMARCH LTD UK

**Journalist:** Alexander, let us start with the simplest question. What exactly is a Genesis Vault artefact? Is it a work of art, a digital file, or a collectible object?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

I would say that the file itself is not the main thing.

Of course, there is a visual work, and it exists as a digital file. But Genesis Vault begins at the point where we stop treating that file simply as an image and start documenting its origin, identity, status, terms of use, and place within a defined collection.

So, for me, a Genesis Vault artefact is first and foremost an **identified cultural object**.

It receives its own identifier, is incorporated into a provenance record, is associated with the relevant visual standard, and may have a cryptographic fingerprint attached to the particular digital file. This makes it possible to verify the integrity of the recorded digital version.

In other words, we are concerned not only with **what the work depicts**, but also with **what kind of object it is, where it came from, and under what status it exists**.

---

## The value of the artefact

**Journalist:** Where, then, does its value come from? There are millions of attractive digital images today.

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

Exactly. That is why aesthetics alone are not enough.

For us, value has several layers.

The first is obviously artistic. We are not trying to register an arbitrary image. Each work is based on a defined artistic and constructive system, a visual standard, and an authorial process.

The second layer is provenance. We want to be able to answer a very simple question several years from now:

**What is this work, and where did it come from?**

The third is institutional fixation. The artefact is not simply uploaded somewhere on the internet. It is incorporated into a defined archival structure, assigned an identifier, and accompanied by documentary information.

And finally, there is the IMARCH school itself. What interests me is the combination of engineering methodology with artistic and symbolic thinking. We are essentially trying to formalise something that normally exists only at the level of an artist's intuition.

---

## Market uniqueness

**Journalist:** But how is that different from ordinary scarcity in the art market? If there are only ten editions, isn't that simply artificially created scarcity?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

If the entire idea were simply to produce ten copies, I would agree with you.

Limited supply by itself creates nothing. You can produce ten thousand completely useless objects and call them rare.

For us, scarcity only becomes meaningful when it exists together with identification and provenance.

Market uniqueness primarily answers one question:

**How much is someone willing to pay for it?**

We are asking a different question:

**What status does this object have, and why should its identity remain meaningful regardless of changes in its price?**

The price may change. Market attention may change. Fashion certainly changes.

But the provenance record, the identifier of the work, its place within the collection, its licensing terms, and its documented history can continue to exist independently of its current market price.

---

## Is it an investment?

**Journalist:** So can Genesis Vault be considered an investment?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

If by investment you mean an expectation of financial return, I would not position it that way.

We deliberately separate collectible value from speculation.

I am more interested in another form of value.

Suppose someone receives an artefact that genuinely means something to them, that they understand, appreciate, and want to own. For that person, it may become part of their cultural environment, professional identity, or institutional profile.

That is a different kind of capital.

I would describe it as **cultural and symbolic capital**.

And it is important not to promise financial returns. We are not offering the buyer a future resale profit. We are offering the object itself, its provenance, its documented status, and the rights that are actually attached to it.

---

## Who is the collector?

**Journalist:** Who, then, is the actual collector you have in mind? Who is this for?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

I would not limit it to people who collect digital art.

I think the more interesting audience is broader.

It could be a private collector who cares not only about the image but also about its provenance.

It could be someone working in architecture, design, technology, education, or cultural research.

It could be a university, a research centre, or another cultural institution.

Because for an organisation, an artefact can function not simply as an artwork, but as **an element of institutional cultural identity**.

An institution might acquire an artefact not because it intends to resell it, but because it wants to demonstrate its participation in a particular intellectual and cultural environment.

---

## What does a university actually gain?

**Journalist:** Let us make that more concrete. What does owning such an artefact actually give a university?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

I would not describe it simply as buying a picture.

If a university becomes the owner or licensee of an artefact, it can incorporate that artefact into its own cultural environment.

Depending on the licence, it might become part of an exhibition, a digital archive, an educational project, a publication, or the visual identity of a particular initiative.

But there is something more important to me.

Such an object can become a starting point for discussing authorship, digital provenance, visual standards, cultural memory, and intellectual property with students.

So the artefact becomes not merely an image, but **a vehicle for education and institutional dialogue**.

---

## An “object of power”

**Journalist:** You have used the word “status” several times. Could Genesis Vault be described as creating a kind of “object of power”?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

If we understand “power” neither literally nor mystically, then yes, I think the expression is useful.

Every culture has objects whose significance goes far beyond their material value. An order, a diploma, an institutional emblem, a work held in a museum collection.

Their value is not determined by their physical material alone.

It emerges from a combination of **provenance, recognition, context, and status**.

Genesis Vault is an attempt to explore that mechanism in a digital environment.

But I would not want to turn it into mysticism. For us, the “power” of an artefact is quite concrete: its identity, provenance, cultural context, documented status, and the rights that are genuinely associated with it.

---

## Then why not simply sell it?

**Journalist:** But these are collectible objects. So they can be purchased?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

Of course. And this is where the interesting paradox begins.

I am not saying that an artefact cannot be bought. There are defined acquisition and licensing models.

But **money is not the only source of its value**.

You can acquire an object, but the purchase itself cannot create its provenance.

You cannot rewrite its history of creation simply by paying for it.

And you cannot simply purchase the cultural role that the object has acquired within a particular system.

That is why I distinguish between **the acquisition price and the value of ownership**.

That distinction is, perhaps, one of the central ideas behind Genesis Vault.

---

## So where does the market fit?

**Journalist:** This creates a rather unusual structure. You say that the market is not the source of value, yet you are offering the artefacts to collectors. How do those two things coexist?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

Quite simply.

The market can be **a mechanism for transferring an object without necessarily being the source of all of its value**.

I can sell a painting. But the artistic history of that painting does not begin at the moment of sale.

I can transfer a document. But its significance does not arise simply because someone paid for the paper.

The same principle applies here.

The market helps establish the price of a particular transaction.

Genesis Vault is attempting to create an infrastructure in which the object also has another kind of life: **provenance, archive, identity, licensing, and cultural context**.

That is much more interesting to me than the price itself.

---

## What is the central idea?

**Journalist:** If you had to explain the entire Genesis Vault concept in one answer, what would you say?

**Oleksandr Purpurov:**

Probably this:

**We are trying to create a way for a digital artwork to have not only an image and a price, but also a documented history.**

A digital object can be copied very easily.

But copying a file and reproducing **its provenance, identity, place within an archive, status, and history of ownership** are two very different things.

That is why, for me, Genesis Vault is not primarily a digital art marketplace. It is an experiment in the infrastructure of digital cultural memory.

And ultimately, the market will tell us how much people actually need this idea.

#### The Consilium Bonus Boutique: the architecture of digital provenance

> **Abstract.** The Consilium Bonus Boutique is not a shop of digital images. It is a prototype of an institution for the accounting of digital property, built on bare open standards and reduced to a minimum of infrastructural dependencies. The article examines the architectural decisions underlying the project: the rejection of the blockchain, the rejection of server infrastructure, the protection of the method instead of the code, the bet on boring standards, and the legal frame of the UK Trade Secrets Regulations 2018. The aim is to show that before us is not an "idea", but a working circuit of provenance, suitable for scaling to other domains.

#### 1. Why this is not a shop

At first glance, the Consilium Bonus Boutique is a display window. Lots, images, buttons, an application form. The standard set of digital commerce.

But if one looks closely at what happens between the click on a lot and the receipt of the master file, the picture changes. The buyer does not "buy a picture". He passes through a procedure of **authentication**, receives a **public node passport**, fixes a **SHA-256 fingerprint**, and enters a **record of the VERDICTUM registry**.

The difference is fundamental. A shop sells a commodity. An institution **attests ownership**. The Consilium Bonus Boutique belongs to the second category.

This places before the architecture a different class of tasks:

- how to guarantee that the file received is the very one that was registered by the author;
- how to confirm authenticity without disclosing the contents;
- how to remove from oneself the storage of personal data;
- how to protect the method, not the particular implementation;
- how to fit all of this into existing law, not into a parallel digital universe.

Below is an analysis of the decisions that were taken on each of these points.

---

#### 2. Hard choice No. 1: provenance without a blockchain

The first thing that comes to mind at the word "provenance" is a blockchain. This is the default solution of the last ten years, and precisely for that reason it is rarely meaningful.

At the basis of "Consilium" lies a different approach: **SHA-256 + a public registry + a notarial passport**.

Every master file is hashed **before** any modification. The hash enters the multi-passport of the series and the public VERDICTUM registry. Any check is a comparison of the locally computed hash with the entry in the registry. Matched — the file is genuine. Not matched — something has happened to the file or to the entry.

What this gives compared with a blockchain:

- **No gas.** No fees. No speculative component.
- **No wallet.** The collector does not need MetaMask to confirm authenticity. A browser is enough.
- **No dependence on the cryptocurrency rate.** The entry threshold is fixed in GBP.
- **No public mempool.** No one sees who is buying what until the moment of publication in the registry.
- **No fork.** No community squabbles, no hard fork, no disputes about governance.

The same verification result, but without the infrastructural and legal circus that the blockchain drags along with it.

This is not a simplification. It is the **rejection of a superfluous layer** that in the task of provenance adds nothing. A hash proves immutability even without a blockchain. A registry proves authorship even without a blockchain. Here the blockchain is a solution in search of a problem.

---

#### 3. Hard choice No. 2: zero-backend and the rejection of data processing

The second decision is more radical than the first. In the system there is **no server-side processing of applications**.

Initially, a Cloudflare Worker was planned: form → worker → Telegram Bot API. That is already not bad: without a database, without storage, in transit. But even so, the data pass through someone else's infrastructure.

Now this is not the case. The scheme is as follows:

1. The user fills in a form on the static site.
2. The data are placed into the **`sessionStorage` of his browser**.
3. The browser redirects him to the second-step page.
4. The page reads the `sessionStorage`, generates a protocol of the application, and shows it to the user.
5. The user copies the protocol and sends it **himself**, from his own email, to the official IMARCH address.

What this means from the point of view of law:

- **Personal data do not leave the user's machine.** Not for a second.
- **GDPR and UK DPA do not apply to IMARCH in respect of these applications.** Because there is nothing to apply — there is no processing.
- **No storage obligations.** No database — no retention periods, no backups, no leaks.
- **No point of failure.** A worker can go down, an API can change, limits can run out. None of this exists.
- **No billing.** There is no service that has to be paid for the form to work.

From the user's point of view — the **same procedure** as before. Filled in, sent, received a reply. The difference is that between the click and the reply there is not a single server that could store, lose or leak anything.

This is not "privacy-first" as a marketing slogan. It is an **architectural guarantee**: IMARCH has no data, because IMARCH does not receive them.

---

#### 4. Hard choice No. 3: protecting the method, not the code

The third layer is legal. It is fixed in the know-how deed **RN-0032**.

The essence of the decision: what falls under protection is not the software code, but the **architectural method**. That is, not "these scripts in Python", but the **set of principles** according to which the system of depositing, verification and distribution of digital objects is built.

What this gives:

- Someone rewrites the scripts from Python to Go — the method is still protected.
- Someone replaces Cloudflare with Fastly — the method is still protected.
- Someone changes SHA-256 to BLAKE3 — the method is still protected.
- Someone redoes the frontend in React — the method is still protected.

What is protected is the **principle**, not the implementation. This is a fundamentally different strategy from the patenting of code. A patent on code is circumvented in a week — it suffices to rewrite a loop in a different structure. Protection of the method holds for years, because what has to be copied is not lines, but **logic**.

In the world of software this is a rare and correct decision. Most projects protect what is easy to copy, and do not protect what is truly valuable.

---

#### 5. Hard choice No. 4: boring standards

The fourth decision is aesthetic. The system is assembled from the **most boring components** that can be found:

- Hugo — a static site generator
- Markdown — a markup language
- SHA-256 — a cryptographic hash
- Cloudflare — CDN and edge hosting
- Telegram Bot API — a notification channel
- ORCID and DOI — academic identifiers
- UK Trade Secrets Regulations 2018 — a statute

Nothing exotic. Nothing "innovative" in the sense of technology. No proprietary blockchains, no home-made protocols, no "we have invented a new standard".

This is a **strength**, not a weakness. Every component:

- has been tested by time;
- is intelligible to the audience;
- is documented;
- has a community;
- requires no explanation.

The innovation lies **in the composition**, not in the components. To assemble a working system of provenance from boring standards is harder than to write one's own blockchain in Rust. Because boring standards do not forgive architectural mistakes: they are not "flexible", they do exactly what is declared. If the composition is wrong — it will not work.

This is the property of mature engineering: not to invent, but **to assemble correctly**.

---

#### 6. Ontology: series → artefact → copy

The fifth decision is structural. It determines **how the catalogue is arranged**.

The system distinguishes three levels:

**Series.** For example, I-1 "Futuristic Ancient Egyptian Cyberpunk". It has a **multi-passport** — a public document describing the visual standard, the composition of the series and the rules of compliance. Address: `RN-0044-PS-0001`.

**Artefact.** A particular work within the series. For example, "Isis in Lapis Lazuli" — artefact I-1.1. It has a **node_id**, a **marker**, a **SHA-256 fingerprint**. Address: `RN-0044-PS-0001-1.1`.

**Copy.** A copy of the artefact within the limited edition. The limit is 10. Each copy receives a **separate annex passport** to the multi-passport. One copy — one owner.

This ontology matters because it **separates the visual standard from the object** and **the object from the copy**. What this gives:

- New series can be issued without rewriting the template.
- The same artefact can be sold in ten copies without confusing them.
- Authenticity can be checked at any level: series, artefact, copy.
- The multi-passport can be published as a separate institutional document.

This is the structure of an **institution**, not of a shop. The difference is like that between a stall and a notary's office.

---

#### 7. The legal circuit: working within the law

The sixth decision is legal. IMARCH does not invent a parallel legal universe. The system works **within existing law**.

The key norm is the **UK Trade Secrets Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/597)**. This is a real statute in force in England and Wales. All the documents of the project — from the know-how deed RN-0032 to the multi-passport RN-0044 — refer precisely to it.

What this means in practice:

- **The documents are recognised by a court.** If it comes to a dispute, a reference to SI 2018/597 is a reference to a norm in force, not to a whitepaper.
- **NDAs have force.** Confidentiality agreements with participants in the ecosystem are backed by the statute.
- **The classification "trade secret" is not a metaphor.** It is a legal fact, not a marketing designation.
- **Cryptographic fixation is evidence.** A SHA-256 fingerprint in the public registry works as a notarial attestation of time and content.

Most digital projects ignore law until the moment when it becomes a problem. IMARCH places law **at the foundation of the architecture** — and this changes everything.

---

#### 8. Self-documentation: the deed as part of the system

The seventh decision is methodological. The documents of the project are **not a description of the system, but a part of it**.

- The know-how deed RN-0032 describes the Zero-Server Gatekeeper → Step-2 in the boutique implements exactly what is described.
- The multi-passport RN-0044 describes the structure of the series → the multi-passport exists as an object.
- The deed on depositing describes the procedure of depositing → the deed itself is deposited according to that procedure.

This is **self-consistency**. Document and implementation do not diverge. The document does not describe a desired state — it describes a **working state** and is itself an example of it.

This is a rare quality. Most projects write manifestos that never coincide with reality. IMARCH writes documents that **are themselves instances** of the procedures described.

---

#### 9. What this gives at scale

If one removes the particulars, what remains is an **infrastructure** suitable for scaling:

- **Domain-agnostic.** The system works not only for images. The same circuit fits documents, audio, 3D models, software code.
- **Jurisdictionally resilient.** The absence of server infrastructure means the absence of attachment to a particular jurisdiction in respect of data.
- **Economically neutral.** No gas, no fees, no speculative component. The entry threshold is fixed.
- **Legally protected.** Every element of the system rests on law in force, not on promises.
- **Reproducible.** The method is reproduced on any stack, if the architectural principles are preserved.

This is not a "startup". It is a **prototype of an institution**. And like any prototype, it is suitable to be shown, licensed, scaled.

---

#### 10. Conclusion

The Consilium Bonus Boutique is not a display window of digital images. It is an **infrastructure of digital provenance**, assembled from boring standards, working without server-side processing of data, protected as a method and not as code, and resting on law in force, not on a parallel universe.

Each of the decisions described above is a **hard choice**. One could have gone the default way: blockchain, AWS, a patent on code, a whitepaper instead of a statute. That would have been faster and more intelligible to the market.

Another path was chosen. A more boring, more reliable, more durable one. One that withstands the test not of a presentation, but of time.

That is precisely why it is an infrastructure and not an idea.

#### 10. Conclusion

The Consilium Bonus Boutique is not a display window of digital images. It is an **infrastructure of digital provenance**, assembled from open and intelligible standards, working without server-side processing of applications, protecting the method and not the particular implementation, and built into the legal circuit in force.

The architecture of the project is built on a series of conscious **hard choices**.

One could have chosen a blockchain, a server-side database, cloud processing of applications, the patenting of the software implementation, or a whitepaper instead of a formalised document. Such a path would have been more customary for the market and, probably, easier to explain at the first level.

Instead, another principle was chosen:

**a minimum of infrastructural dependencies, a maximum of verifiability of procedure.**

There is no proprietary cryptocurrency here, because for the task of provenance it is not required.

There is no server-side database, because the procedure can be organised without centralised storage of user data.

There is no binding of the method to a particular software stack, because the value lies in the architecture, not in the lines of code.

There is no need to create a proprietary technological mythology, because the system rests on already existing standards, identifiers, cryptographic mechanisms and legal instruments.

As a result, what becomes innovative is not an individual component, but **their composition**.

It is precisely here that the boundary runs between a digital product and an infrastructure. A product can be shown. An infrastructure can be verified, reproduced and applied again.

Therefore "Consilium" should be regarded not as a finished answer to the problem of digital property, but as a **working prototype of an institutional circuit of provenance**.

Its principal value lies in the reproducibility of the architecture.

If the logic of series, artefact and copy is preserved, together with the mechanism of cryptographic fixation, the procedure of public verification, the documentary circuit and the corresponding legal grounds, the same approach can be transferred to other types of digital objects: documents, audio recordings, 3D models, software code and other forms of digital work.

Thus the possibility arises of scaling not a display window and not a collection of images, but **the very principle of institutional accounting and verification of digital objects**.

And in this sense "Consilium" is of interest no longer as an experiment of an individual boutique.

It is a **prototype of an infrastructure capable of becoming an institution**.
