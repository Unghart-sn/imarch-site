#### The Problem: What Happens After the Mission Formally Ends

When a service member returns home or a person retires after many years of work, on the outside it looks like “demobilisation” or a “well‑earned rest.”

Inside, however, a different process often begins: **“deactivation stress.”**

The person loses their familiar rhythm, their team, the structure of the day, and the sense of being needed.

Added to this are traumatic memories, bodily tension, fear of the future, and the family’s hidden exhaustion.

---

#### 🧠 Chronic Stress and PTSD

For many veterans and their loved ones, reality looks like this:

- intrusive memories and flashbacks;
- anxiety, irritability, outbursts of anger;
- sleep disturbances, nightmares, fatigue that “no amount of sleep can fix”;
- physical symptoms: pain, muscle spasms, problems with blood pressure, breathing, digestion.

Even when the person is officially “home,” the psyche continues to live in threat mode.

---

#### 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 The Burden on the Family

It is not only the veteran who suffers, but also those around them:

- the partner or spouse lives in constant tension;
- children intuitively sense the adults’ state but do not understand its causes;
- relationships shift into survival mode rather than growth.

The family simultaneously becomes the **sole front of support**  
and the **area of greatest strain**.

Without accessible tools for “home, for family,” this burden only increases.

---

#### 🧩 Inaccessibility and Fragmentation of Help

Even when help formally exists, barriers arise in practice:

- quality psychotherapy and rehabilitation are often too expensive;
- in many regions, specialists with the right profile simply do not exist;
- stigma prevents people from “going to a psychologist” or “complaining about weakness”;
- most programmes are not integrated into the family’s daily life.

The result:

- some veterans never reach help at all;
- some drop out of programmes because they see no result;
- some remain in a state of “frozen crisis” for years.

---

#### 🔄 The Vicious Circle

Without systemic support, a typical scenario unfolds:

1. The veteran returns or leaves the system.
2. Stress and tension accumulate but are not processed.
3. The family burns out, irritation and distance grow.
4. Associated problems increase: addictions, conflicts, exacerbation of chronic illnesses.
5. The support system intervenes late and sporadically, but does not change the underlying pattern.

This creates a **hidden wave of losses** – people who have physically survived but gradually drop out of active life.

---

#### 🕒 Why Action Is Needed Now

This problem will not “resolve itself.”  
Each year of delay:

- increases the number of people with chronic psychosomatic conditions;
- raises the burden on healthcare and social support systems;
- deepens families’ sense of helplessness;
- undermines trust in the institutions that “should have helped.”

If we do not offer **simple, accessible, and reproducible support practices**,  
we risk losing an entire generation of people who possess experience, strength, and potential.

---

#### 💬 What Needs to Change

The problem is not only a lack of resources, but also the format of help.

What is needed are solutions that:

- work **within the family and community**, not only in a specialist’s office;
- rely on **somatic and psycho‑emotional practices** that are understandable and reproducible;
- deliver quick, tangible results so that people begin to believe in change;
- are embedded in a **transparent system of tracking and feedback**, so donors and partners can see the real effect.
